# Study and analysis limitations

These points are for the thesis limitations discussion; they do not change the approved analysis definitions or results.

- **Incomplete counterbalancing.** The six realised condition sequences balanced total exposure and spread NT across trial positions, but full counterbalancing across all condition combinations and orders was not feasible because the study could not recruit the additional participants within the available time. Ideally, every participant would have experienced all three animated conditions and the no-animation condition, allowing within-participant comparisons across the full set.

- **Unequal detection windows.** Supervisor comment: “So onset window is 60 s and completion is 30 s. Could explain why onset was detected more than completion. You can acknowledge this as limitation later.” The eligible onset window is 30 to before 90 s; completion is 90 to before 120 s. The difference in opportunity may contribute to the observed detection counts, but these data do not isolate its effect.
- **Controlled simulator setting.** The study took place in a controlled simulator rather than on real roads. This supports a consistent study setting but limits how directly the findings may generalise to real-world driving.
- **Restricted gaze instruction.** Participants were prompted not to look at the display and to keep their eyes on the road. This differs from naturalistic driving, where drivers may choose to glance at a display, and limits generalisation of the attention findings.
- **Fixed speed display.** The driver axis did not show real-time speed. Participants instead saw a fixed 50 km/h value and received researcher speed prompts; this differs from a live vehicle display and limits ecological validity.
- **Q1/Q2 priming of oral interpretation.** Participants answered the Q1/Q2 discrimination questions before describing what they witnessed and what they thought it represented. The available answer options, and the options they selected, may have shaped or reinforced those later accounts. They may also have constrained participants’ responses to Q1/Q2 themselves: a participant who was uncertain, or whose understanding did not match any option exactly, may have chosen the closest available wording or metaphor. The oral responses therefore cannot be treated as fully independent, spontaneous evidence of what participants perceived or inferred, and questionnaire answers may not fully capture participants’ own descriptions; the extent and direction of these influences were not measured. The question order and existing validity concern are documented in [Methods: Data Preparation, Analysis, Validity and Scope](../context/methods_data-preparation.md).

- **Post hoc inferential selection.** The [adopted Chapter 4 tests](inference_ch4.md) were chosen after prior analyses were inspected; their separate Holm families do not control the full historical search. Rerun validation checks arithmetic and provenance, not confirmatory status or external validity.
- **Confidence scale and paired interpretation overlap.** Numerical confidence means treat the four recorded steps as equally spaced and do not test medians or calibration. Each between-animation interpretation test uses eight shared participants; full-condition descriptive groups of 16 are not the matched inferential denominator. Coding remains provisional and single-coder. See the current results and validation.

## Limitations and future-work points moved from the Discussion

These notes retain the learning paragraph and follow-up questions removed from the chat draft on 20 October 2026. Proposed studies and interactions below have not been carried out.

- **Learning and personal involvement were not evaluated.** Participants had not chosen the visual meanings and had not been taught what the animations represented. The study therefore examined how readily they understood unfamiliar representations. The driving familiarisation introduced the trigger task, not the intended animation meanings; see the [study procedure](../context/methods_study-methodology.md#353-procedure). Future evaluations could compare first encounters with explicit introductions and repeated use, distinguishing unfamiliar meanings from meanings that remain difficult to learn; the present study does not establish whether learning would make these mappings effective during driving.

- **Object changes and whole-scene changes were not compared independently.** The Discussion proposes that an identifiable object may be easier to follow than a gradual camera zoom, but the animations also differed in content, colour, movement, timing and intended meaning. Their performance cannot isolate the effect of any one feature or establish a general ranking of abstraction. A controlled comparison could hold the message and scene constant while varying whether the update changes one object's colour, size or position, or the viewpoint over the whole scene. Separately varying speed, spatial extent and repetition could clarify how they affect recognition and attention. This proposal follows the observations and the [methodological limits on comparing conditions](../context/methods_data-preparation.md#37-validity-reliability-and-scope).

- **Immediate metaphor clarity and competing meanings need separate evaluation.** Familiar imagery did not always communicate its intended purpose: a correct visual description could accompany an alternative meaning. Candidate metaphors could be compared before detailed animation development by asking what information or function each suggests, including unintended meanings, then examining whether that reading survives the driving task. This is a proposed design evaluation based on the oral interpretation findings, not an additional completed study.

- **Returning from an update to an idle state was not tested as an interactive cycle.** No-animation trials used a static shared scene; the study did not establish that returning to it after an update reduces attention or signals that acknowledgement is no longer needed. Nor did it test whether a driver acknowledgement, driving action or loss of relevance should end an update. These are proposed interactions for evaluating the idle–update–settled-state design. Preserve the distinction between recognising no change after driving and withholding a trigger press: 18/24 no-animation questionnaire responses correctly reported no change, whereas 8/24 trials contained false-alarm presses. These counts do not establish an unambiguous or attention-free baseline. See the [condition and procedure descriptions](../context/methods_study-methodology.md), recognition results and no-animation response findings.

- **Gaze does not establish why participants looked or whether looking caused understanding.** Continued inspection could help resolve uncertainty, or uncertainty could persist despite additional looking. Small outcome groups, incomplete gaze coverage and intervals containing time after trigger presses restrict those explanations. The additional exploratory gaze–recognition/interpretation comparison discussed in this chat remains outside the adopted Chapter 4 inference; inconclusive associations do not establish no relationship. Follow-up work could use better gaze coverage and event-aligned measures to examine when inspection occurs relative to recognition or reporting. Neither display-looking time nor gaze away from the display establishes exclusively peripheral understanding or continuous attention to the road. See the gaze and detection observations, [methods and validity boundaries](../context/methods_data-preparation.md#37-validity-reliability-and-scope) and [adopted inferential scope](inference_ch4.md).
