\begin{scontents}[store-env=abstracts,print-env=true]
As vehicle displays grow, more information competes for drivers’ attention. Ambient lighting has been explored as a way to communicate information unobtrusively to drivers, while non-urgent information about the surrounding environment remains less explored. We propose that this contextual information could be better represented through visual metaphors, which allow richer encodings through imagery, colour, shape and movement. This study explores their use on BMW’s Panoramic Vision, which offers extensive space for peripheral interaction and could support awareness of the surrounding context. A workshop with $n = 13$ participants informed three animations conveying different messages within an aesthetically pleasing scene. These were evaluated alongside a static no-animation condition in a driving simulator with $n = 24$ participants. We examined how participants detected changes while driving, recognised the displayed content afterwards, and interpreted the visual stimuli. Participants noticed ongoing changes more often than their completion. They also recognised displayed elements more often than the changes themselves, while interpretation of the animations’ meanings varied. The findings suggest that ambient displays need understandable links between visual elements and messages, alongside a clear distinction between ongoing updates and settled states. They provide a basis for developing a shared visual language that could help larger displays keep drivers informed while respecting the attention needed for the road.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Vehicle dashboards increasingly feature larger displays extending beyond instrument clusters and central screens, as illustrated by BMW's Panoramic Vision \cite{BMWPanoramicIDrive2025}. This creates space for navigation, vehicle status and entertainment, but poorly designed content can compete for drivers' attention. Reading or repeatedly checking content can draw attention away from driving \cite{lambleDetectionThresholdsCar1999}, even near the windscreen \cite{hauslschmidAugmentingDriversView2015}.

The additional space also offers opportunities for peripheral interaction: cues at the edge of the driver's view could support awareness of the surrounding context without requiring direct inspection \cite{huangCharacteristicsDriverPeripheral2025}. The challenge is to communicate information through changes that remain perceptible and understandable despite peripheral vision's limited resolution for fine details and complex patterns \cite{strasburgerPeripheralVisionPattern2011}.

Peripheral communication in cars has largely been explored through ambient lighting \cite{lockenEnlighteningDriversSurvey2016}, while communicating non-urgent contextual information, such as weather and characteristics of the surrounding environment, remains less explored. Panoramic Vision already offers weather, navigation and music widgets alongside the instrument cluster \cite{BMWPanoramicIDrive2025}. We propose visual metaphors as a richer approach to encoding contextual information, combining recognisable imagery with changes in colour, shape, movement and spatial relationships.

Beyond communicating information, these representations could help manufacturers strengthen the vehicle's aesthetic identity and shape the sensory character of its interior \cite{tractinsky2011slow,loehmannHeartbeatExperiencePulse2014}. Offering choices of visual themes and imagery could also allow drivers to personalise the interior's digital appearance to suit their preferences, potentially strengthening their emotional attachment to the vehicle over time.

Grounded in calm technology \cite{weiserDesigningCalmTechnology,bakkerDesignPeripheralInteraction2013}, this work introduces an aesthetic ambient display featuring an immersive 3D scene that communicates data through dynamic visual changes. An exploratory workshop with $n = 13$ BMW employees informed three metaphor-based animations: sun-to-moon for time of day, buildings for the surrounding location and terrain, and a red sphere for progress towards a destination. Twenty-four participants evaluated the animations on an A-pillar-to-A-pillar Panoramic Vision display in a driving simulator, each experiencing no-animation and two animated trials.

The study addressed three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How effectively do drivers detect changes in a peripheral ambient display, and how much visual attention does the display receive?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} How accurately and confidently can drivers recognise which element was shown and what kind of change occurred?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} How do drivers interpret the intended meaning of metaphor-based visual changes in a peripheral ambient display?
\end{itemize}

Participants reported ongoing changes more often than their completion, although the reporting windows differed in length. They recognised displayed elements more accurately and confidently than their visual changes. Understanding also varied by metaphor: the intended meaning was identified most often for buildings, less often for sun-to-moon and never for red-sphere in the observed trials. Recognising an element therefore did not guarantee understanding its message (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

The thesis makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1:} An aesthetic ambient display with three implemented metaphor-based animations for an A-pillar to A-pillar panoramic dashboard.
  \item \textbf{C2:} Evidence about drivers' detection, recognition and interpretation of these animations under simulator conditions.
  \item \textbf{C3:} An adapted evaluation approach for automotive aesthetic ambient displays that distinguishes detection of visual changes, recognition of displayed elements and changes, and interpretation of their meaning, extending Holmquist's comprehension framework \cite{holmquistEvaluatingComprehensionAmbient2004}.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

\section{Calm Technology}
\label{sec:calm-technolog}

Calm technology aims to inform without continually demanding focused attention. It keeps information at the edge of awareness, where it remains perceptible, and brings it to the foreground when a change or its relevance warrants attention \cite{weiserDesigningCalmTechnology}.

This approach reflects how vision works. The fovea, at the centre of our gaze, supports detailed recognition, while the wider peripheral field is more sensitive to motion and changes in brightness \cite{strasburgerPeripheralVisionPattern2011,stewartReviewInteractionsPeripheral2020} \cite{zhaopingPeripheralVisionMainly2024}.

Information in peripheral vision can draw attention either by standing out (\textit{e.g. a sudden change in intensity}) or by aligning with our goals and expectations \cite{bakkerDesignPeripheralInteraction2013}. How much attention such cues require becomes crucial when multitasking, as limited cognitive capacity must be divided among activities \cite{wickensMultipleResourcesPerformance2002}. Fortunately, monitoring peripheral information is significantly less resource-intensive than engaging in controlled tasks (\textit{e.g. reading, understanding and interpreting text}) \cite{bakkerDesignPeripheralInteraction2013,heijboerFacilitatingPeripheralInteraction2016,bakkerPeripheralInteractionCharacteristics2015}.

\section{Peripheral Interaction}
\label{sec:peripheral-interaction}

Peripheral interaction builds on the principle of calm technology by allowing information to shift fluidly between the periphery and the centre of attention, enabling users to engage more deeply whenever necessary \cite{weiserComingAgeCalmTechnology1997,bakkerPeripheralInteractionShaping2014}. This transition between states depends on both design and context: while familiarity allows an interaction to remain unobtrusive in the background, factors like task difficulty, information urgency, or a prominent visual cue can pull it into focus \cite{bakkerInteractionAttentionContinuumConsidering,bakkerPeripheralInteractionCharacteristics2015}. In driving, peripheral vision supports lane keeping and awareness of the surroundings, but can be limited by cognitive load and distraction \cite{vaterPeripheralVisionRealworld2022}.

Designing for peripheral interaction consequently requires considering what users need from a display while carrying out their main activity. Matthews and colleagues use Activity Theory to guide this decision: the display should provide information relevant to that activity, with greater emphasis on changes whose consequences make them important to notice \cite{matthewsDefiningDesigningEvaluating2007}. Glanceable displays aim to make this information understandable through brief looks, reducing the need to interrupt the main task \cite{matthewsDesigningEvaluatingGlanceable2006}. Designers can support this by simplifying the information (\textit{abstraction}), controlling how strongly it attracts attention (\textit{notification level}), and choosing how changes appear (\textit{transitions}) \cite{matthewsToolkitManagingUser2004,matthewsDesigningEvaluatingGlanceable2006}. These decisions also shape how much information an ambient display communicates and how it fits into its surroundings: Pousman and Stasko describe this balance through information capacity, notification level, representational fidelity, and aesthetic emphasis \cite{pousmanTaxonomyAmbientInformation2006}.

\section{Ambient Displays}
\label{sec:ambient-displays-applications}

Ambient displays are designed to communicate information
at the periphery of users' attention, embodying the
principles of calm technology. These systems employ a range of communication modalities, including visual (light, colour, projected imagery and graphical representations), auditory (non-speech sounds and sonification), kinetic (physical movement
of objects), haptic (vibration and temperature) and olfactory (scent-based signals). Among these, visual communication remains the dominant modality, with ambient lighting output being the most widely adopted approach \cite{SheltonNesbitt2020}.

\setcounter{secnumdepth}{2}

\subsection{In-Car Ambient Lighting}
\label{sec:automotive-ambient-displays}

Ambient lighting research in driving assistance focuses primarily on lane-change support and hazard visualisation, with secondary applications in speed regulation, gaze orientation, and vehicle status display \cite{lockenEnlighteningDriversSurvey2016}.

Across these domains, ambient cues effectively contextualise information spatially and temporally. They direct driver gaze towards key road zones \cite{pomarjanschiSimpleGazeContingentCues2013}, induce earlier braking \cite{laquaiLargeScaleLEDArray2011}, and widen safety margins \cite{lockenSupportingLaneChange2015}. Compared with graphical navigation displays, light-based signals foster shorter and less frequent glances \cite{matviienkoNaviLightInvestigatingAmbient2016}.

However, light cues can be visually imprecise or distracting. Intricate patterns are difficult to memorise and interpret without prior familiarity with colour coding and driving context, often requiring supplementary information in complex situations \cite{lockenEnlighteningDriversSurvey2016,matviienkoDerivingDesignGuidelines2015,matviienkoNaviLightInvestigatingAmbient2016}.

Current research focuses largely on ambient lighting for immediate driving manoeuvres, leaving open how ambient communication might support broader contextual awareness of non-urgent information.

\subsection{Aesthetic Awareness Displays}
\label{sec:aesthetic-awareness-displays}

A discussed application within these categories is using ambient displays to inform drivers of external conditions, like the weather \cite{lockenAdaptiveAmbientInVehicle2017}. For this purpose, traditional ambient lighting offers limited expressiveness due to its reliance on a restricted set of encoding parameters, such as colour, brightness, and LED position \cite{matviienkoNewAmbientLight2015}. In contrast, aesthetic awareness displays \cite{sheltonAestheticAwarenessDisplay2016} enable richer information representation by employing visual metaphors based on colour, space, shape, abstraction, dimension, and orientation \cite{ferschaInformativeArtDisplay2007} to map underlying data to visual changes.

For example, Holmquist and Skog developed a Mondrian-inspired weather display using coloured squares to represent weather data across six cities, with square position identifying the city, size indicating temperature, and colour conveying weather conditions \cite{holmquistInformativeArtInformation2003}. Similarly, the InfoCanvas platform allowed users to personalise peripheral visual scenes, in which individual elements encoded relevant information, such as a bird’s flying height representing stock market trends \cite{staskoPersonalizedPeripheralInformation2004}. Skog et al.\ also developed a Mondrian-inspired display to communicate bus departure information, where each square represented a bus, its size decreased as departure approached, its colour indicated the urgency of leaving, and its position conveyed the direction of travel \cite{skogAestheticsUtilityDesigning2003}.

A key challenge of such visualisations lies in the difficulty users may face in interpreting abstract metaphors without explicit guidance \cite{holmquistInformativeArtInformation2003}. Evaluating such displays must distinguish whether viewers can identify what information is represented from whether they can interpret its visual representation correctly \cite{skogAestheticsUtilityDesigning2003}. In automotive contexts, assessments must also establish whether drivers can detect unobtrusive visual changes while maintaining their attention on the road ahead.

While visual metaphors have been explored to promote eco-driving \cite{tractinsky2011slow,dahlinger2018impact,potvinBernalInfluencingGreaterAdoption2020}, their potential for peripheral ambient communication remains underexplored.

\subsection{BMW Panoramic Vision}
\label{sec:bmw-panoramic-idrive-and-panoramic-vision}

\begin{evidenceblock}
\normalsize
BMW's Panoramic Vision provides extensive space for peripheral interaction and shows promise in enhancing drivers' situational awareness \cite{huangCharacteristicsDriverPeripheral2025}. The display projects the instrument cluster and configurable widgets onto a black-printed area at the base of the windscreen, spanning from A-pillar to A-pillar \cite{BMWPanoramicIDrive2025} (Figure~\ref{fig:bmw-panoramic-idrive}). Prior research favoured the below-windscreen position over other cockpit locations for ambient communication due to its proximity to the driver's line of sight \cite{lockenInVehicleAmbientLight2019}, particularly when displaying persistent information \cite{mirnigEnlighteningModeAwareness2023}.

\section{Chapter Synthesis}
\label{sec:chapter-synthesis}

Ambient communication of non-urgent contextual information, such as weather, remains underexplored. Aesthetic displays offer richer encodings than ambient lighting, but their automotive use requires evaluating whether drivers can detect changes, recognise visual metaphors and interpret their meaning while driving. Panoramic Vision provides an expansive display area close to the driver's line of sight for exploring this approach.

\chapter{Design and Evaluation Methodology}
\label{ch:methods}

Driven by an exploratory workshop on driver preferences for ambient information design, the study informed the creation of a panoramic vision prototype comprising three visual stimuli and a baseline condition, which was evaluated in a controlled driving simulator.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:study-design}

Participants drove the simulator as their primary task while reporting display changes. Each participant completed three trials: one non-animated and two with different animations, resulting in $n = 24$ non-animated trials and $n = 16$ per animated condition (for a total of $n = 72$ trials). Six sequences were assigned to four participants each, so everyone saw both animated and non-animated visuals (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-condition-sequences}). Full counterbalancing was not feasible within the recruitment period, resulting in red-sphere being the first condition in two sequences and the first animation encountered in all four sequences that included it.

The peripheral detection task (PDT) typically adds a separate cue-detection task alongside driving, using response times and missed cues to assess workload and distraction \cite{harmsPeripheralDetectionMeasure2003}. Here, we adapt the button-response procedure to assess whether drivers notice ambient animations starting and stopping, rather than using separate cues to infer workload.

\subsection{Apparatus}
\label{sec:apparatus}

\begin{evidenceblock}
\normalsize
The setup included a driving-simulator cockpit running on BMW's SPIDER framework, the panoramic vision, a phone mounted centrally on the steering wheel, an iPad used by the researcher and a Tobii Pro Spark eye tracker operating at 60~Hz, mounted on the dashboard in front of the driver. The prototype was built in Spline \cite{splineSplineOfficial} and ran on the simulator PC, which streamed it directly to the panoramic display. Both the phone on the steering wheel and the researcher iPad ran separate ProtoPie projects. The phone recorded presses for the PDT, while the iPad controlled the trial timer, started and stopped gaze recording, and recorded up to five received PDT triggers per trial (see Figure~\ref{fig:protopie-interfaces}). A Python script using the Tobii software development kit (SDK) controlled gaze recording. Figure~\ref{fig:simulator-setup} shows the setup.

\subsection{Participants}
\label{sec:participants}

We initially recruited $n = 27$ participants. One session was excluded as pilot, while two formal sessions were excluded because they exceeded the system’s limit of five reliably recorded PDT triggers per trial, leaving $n = 24$ participants for analysis. The design target of $n = 24$ participants was a multiple of six matching the six stimuli sequences, within a 7-working-day window of approximately 5 hours per day.

Participants were aged 21--45 years and included more men than women; all were BMW staff, and some had also taken part in the workshop. Annual driving distance ranged from 0 to 50,000~km ($M = 11{,}146$~km, $SD = 10{,}373$~km). Most had previous experience with driving simulators, some used vision correction and nobody reported colour-vision deficiency (Table~\ref{tab:demographics}).

\section{Data Preparation and Analysis}
\label{sec:data-analysis}




Lane position, NASA-TLX and UEQ provide supplementary context rather than direct answers to the three research questions (\S\ref{sec:additional-results}).

\subsection{Detection}
\label{sec:detection}

We aligned each PDT press with the 120 s timeline and classified it using the categories in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:rq1-trigger-classifications}.

 
For animated trials, detection success was calculated separately for onset (30–90~s) and completion (90–120~s) as the number of matched detections within each window divided by the number of eligible trials. Rates were averaged into a single score only when comparing against non-animated trials or summarising all trials. Scores were assigned as 1 for full detection (both 30–90~s and 90–120~s windows), 0.5 for partial detection (one window), and 0 for none. For non-animated trials, a trial with
no press was counted as successful (1). Onset (30--90~s) and completion (90--120~s) detection were compared using a two-sided paired $t$-test on $n = 24$ participants' mean scores across their two animated trials, retaining misses as zero. To account for multiple testing, Holm correction was applied jointly to this comparison and 21 other comparisons involving detection, recognition accuracy and interpretation across all trials, animated trials and no-animation trials (see Table~\ref{tab:holm-detection-family}). Within each animation, two-sided exact McNemar tests compared $n = 16$ paired onset and completion scores, with Holm adjustment across the three animations.

We then defined reaction time as \(RT = t_{\mathrm{press}} - t_{\mathrm{anchor}}\), where \(t_{\mathrm{anchor}}\in\{30,90\}\)~s, and summarised it descriptively among detected
events. Presses not matched to an onset or completion detection were analysed separately using descriptive statistics.  Trigger events, reaction times, and non-detection presses were compared across all trials ($n = 72$), between animated ($n = 48$) vs. non-animated ($n = 24$) conditions, and by time window within animated trials.

\subsection{Gaze}
\label{sec:gaze}

We retained gaze records within the 0--120~s trial interval. Valid samples inside the \emph{\(3{,}840 \times 656\) px} canvas were classified as \emph{driver-axis} when \(0 \leq X < 1{,}280\)~px or \emph{ambient-display} when \(1{,}280 \leq X < 3{,}840\)~px. Samples outside this frame or marked unclassified were labelled off-display, while intervals without samples remained missing.

An \emph{ambient contact} was a continuous sequence of valid ambient-display samples no more than 25~ms apart. At 60~Hz, samples were expected every 16.7~ms; a 25~ms tolerance allowed timing variation, while longer gaps remained unassigned \cite{niehorster_fundamentals_2026}.

For intervals up to 25~ms, we placed any change between gaze regions halfway between the two recorded times, because its exact timing between observations was unknown \cite{andersson_sampling_2010}. No minimum contact duration or fixation classification was applied; contacts therefore represented recorded segments rather than confirmed intentional looks.

Contacts were constructed before retaining fixed windows: 30--90~s and 90--120~s for animated trials, and 0--120~s for no-animation trials. Animated windows retained detected and missed changes, including gaze after presses.

Coverage was checked separately for each window using only valid ambient-display and driver-axis samples:
\[
\text{Coverage} =
\frac{N_{\text{ambient}} + N_{\text{driver-axis}}}
{T_{\text{window}} \times 60}
\]
Here, \(T_{\text{window}}\) is duration in seconds (expected samples: \(3{,}600\) for 30--90~s; \(1{,}800\) for 90--120~s; \(7{,}200\) for 0--120~s), following \cite{niehorster_fundamentals_2026}. Off-display samples did not contribute to the numerator. Windows required at least 80\% coverage and positive allocated valid duration; otherwise, they were classified as indeterminate.

\normalsize
Total ambient gaze time and contact frequency (Table~\ref{tab:gaze-allocation-measures}) were compared descriptively, with frequency expressed per minute of the full window. Together, they distinguished fewer, longer recorded contacts from more frequent, shorter ones when cumulative time and window length were comparable. Adequately covered windows without contacts retained zero values.

\subsection{Recognition Accuracy and Confidence}
\label{sec:recognition-accuracy-confidence}

We scored element recognition (Q1) and change recognition (Q2) as correct (1) or incorrect (0), using the questionnaire answer key (Table~\ref{tab:app-q1-q2-answer-key}), and reported correct-answer counts, percentages and mean accuracy scores---the proportion correct; two-sided paired $t$-tests compared Q1 and Q2 participant means across all $n = 72$ trials and the $n = 48$ animated trials ($n = 24$ participant pairs per comparison; Holm adjustment), while exact McNemar tests compared paired answers within each condition ($n = 24$ no-animation pairs and $n = 16$ per animation; Holm adjustment across four tests).

Confidence ratings ranged from 1 (\emph{guessing}) to 4 (\emph{very sure}) and were summarised descriptively by question, condition and correctness; two-sided paired $t$-tests compared Q1 and Q2 using $n = 24$ participant means overall and for animated trials, and paired trial ratings within each condition ($n = 24$ no-animation pairs and $n = 16$ per animation), with Holm adjustment.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}
We used thematic analysis to compare every participant's oral interpretation of visual metaphors with their intended meaning while retaining additional details (Tables~\ref{tab:rq3-meaning-codebook} and~\ref{tab:rq3-detail-codebook}).

Each trial received one main \texttt{success} outcome, marked \texttt{full} or \texttt{partial} depending on how clearly the intended meaning was expressed, or \texttt{failure}. Success meant identifying time passing in the sun-to-moon animation, location or surroundings in the buildings animation, destination progress in the red-sphere animation, or recognising that no meaningful animation occurred in a non-animated trial, while failures were coded as an \texttt{alternative meaning} (a different interpretation), \texttt{unknown} (no meaning offered), or \texttt{false-change} (reporting a visual change that did not happen). 

Coding retained additional details such as which element from the scene was described (\texttt{object}), the visual cue witnessed (\texttt{transformation}), point in the animation when it was noticed (\texttt{stage}), meaning assigned (\texttt{interpretation}), link between a visual cue and its meaning (\texttt{reason}), confidence about the meaning (\texttt{certainty}) and stated reaction (\texttt{reaction}).

We summarised outcomes and response details descriptively by condition, counting full and partial success as 1 and failure as 0, while qualitatively reporting additional response details.

\subsection{Additional Results}
\label{sec:additional-results}

The following measures provide supplementary context on driving behaviour, workload and user experience. Summaries are reported in Section~\ref{sec:add-results}, with details in the appendices referenced below; they are secondary to the three research questions.

Absolute distance from the lane centre \(d = |\texttt{laneOffsetLeft} + \texttt{laneOffsetRight}|/2\), was compared across conditions and press behaviours, (see Appendix ~\ref{sec:driving-behaviour}).

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Participants often reported animation onset and recognised the displayed elements, while the intended meaning was identified in only half of the animated trials (Tables~\ref{tab:rq1-detection-latency}, \ref{tab:rq2-condition-accuracy} and~\ref{tab:rq3-interpretation-counts}). The central design difficulty was connecting the visible elements and their changes with the information they represented.

\section{Communicating meaning through visual metaphors}
\label{sec:discussion-meaning}

The scene provided recognisable symbols, but their intended meanings were not always accessible through their appearance or behaviour \cite{ferschaInformativeArtDisplay2007}. This difficulty was particularly evident in the red-sphere animation, where participants usually recognised the sphere, rarely recognised its progressive camera close-up and never identified destination progress in the observed trials (Table~\ref{tab:animation-outcomes}). Their explanations instead emphasised colour, glow and pulse, sometimes associating these features with warnings or a driving mode. Previous research describes associations of red with danger and its use to signal a need to act, consistent with these warning-related interpretations \cite{ferschaMatterTaste2007,matviienkoNewAmbientLight2015}.

The choice of symbol may consequently have placed too much interpretative weight on the transition. Understanding destination progress required participants both to recognise the camera close up as an approach and to interpret the sphere as its endpoint; recognising the sphere alone provided little support for that connection. The slow transition may have made the camera close up difficult to distinguish during brief views. Background variation can make an attended figure harder to perceive and interpret \cite{ferschaMatterTaste2007}; in this prototype, the reverse may also have occurred. The sphere's colour, upward movement, glow and surrounding particles may have made the scene's approach harder to distinguish, leaving participants with a recognisable object but without the movement needed to interpret destination progress.

For this prototype, we propose that the main symbol convey the message while movement and background changes reinforce it, helping users connect what they see with what it means \cite{ferschaMatterTaste2007}. A destination marker such as a pin or flag would offer an alternative symbol to test, potentially making the destination association available even when the approach is missed.

The sun-to-moon animation illustrates how several visual changes can reinforce the same transition. Horizontal movement and changes in the sphere's and background's colours may together explain its prompt detection and successful recognition, making the transition apparent through several features of a familiar day-to-night sequence. Participants recognised this change more often than the buildings transition, where gradual vertical movement may have produced less distinguishable differences during brief views (Table~\ref{tab:animation-outcomes}). This suggests a role for coordinated changes across the symbol and background in making an update perceptible, consistent with the importance of visible activity in ambient displays \cite{skogAestheticsUtilityDesigning2003}.

However, this recognition advantage did not translate into more successful interpretation: the buildings animation was interpreted correctly more often than the more literal sun-to-moon animation. The buildings' recognisable form may have provided a useful connection to surroundings even when their movement was missed, supporting the value of linking visual cues to the information they represent \cite{skogAestheticsUtilityDesigning2003}. The symbol should show what the information is about, while movement and background changes help users notice when it changes.

\section{Distinguishing updates from stable states}
\label{sec:discussion-updates}

The display should clearly distinguish an idle scene, an ongoing update, and a completed change. Although every participant recognised the no-animation scene, some still reported changes, possibly reflecting uncertainty under the reporting instructions. Median looking time was similar with and without animation, while recorded contact frequency was slightly higher with animation (Tables~\ref{tab:gaze-whole-trial-time} and~\ref{tab:gaze-whole-trial-frequency}), showing that the absence of animation did not eliminate looking at the display.

Continued sparkling and gradual stopping points may also have obscured completion. During the 90--120-second completion window, median looking time was longer without a completion report than with one (Table~\ref{tab:gaze-fixed-summaries}), possibly reflecting continued checking. Transitions should therefore be perceptible during brief views and settle clearly when the information becomes stable, extending the balance between visible activity and excessive fluctuation to the recognition of endpoints \cite{skogAestheticsUtilityDesigning2003}. Holding the updated scene still could help distinguish completion from an ongoing update.

\section{Adapting ambient information to driving}
\label{sec:discussion-driving}

These difficulties arose during a single brief encounter with each animation, leaving open whether familiarity would improve understanding. For driving, however, we argue that information needed during a journey should be understandable from first use, in contrast to the view that immediate comprehension is unnecessary for informative art \cite{holmquistInformativeArtInformation2003}.

The gaps between detection, recognition and interpretation support evaluating these outcomes separately, because success in one did not guarantee success in another. This approach adapts the comprehension framework proposed by Holmquist \cite{holmquistEvaluatingComprehensionAmbient2004}. Participants were already told that Panoramic Vision conveyed information, so the present study explicitly assessed detection of changes while driving rather than discovery of the display's informative role. We propose detectability as an additional evaluation dimension: contextual information should remain perceptually available so that drivers can notice an update and decide whether it warrants further attention according to their immediate goals. Assessing recognition and interpretation alongside detection then reveals difficulties that would remain hidden if noticing a change were taken as evidence of understanding its message.

Beyond recognising an update and its meaning, drivers may need more detail than an ambient overview provides. A broad impression of time passing may support routine awareness, but a driver concerned about arriving late may need the exact time or remaining journey duration. Although the informative-art account allows users to obtain detail from other sources, we argue that an in-car overview should be accompanied by access to precise information within the vehicle interface whenever the task requires it, rather than depend on consulting a separate source \cite{holmquistInformativeArtInformation2003}. Requests for greater precision in earlier ambient-display use reinforce this requirement \cite{staskoPersonalizedPeripheralInformation2004}: the overview can remain visually simple, while exact information remains available when needed.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study setup limits how directly the findings reflect spontaneous awareness in everyday driving. Participants drove in a simulator, were instructed to avoid looking at the display, and received both a fixed speed value and spoken speed prompts. Asking participants to report changes with a trigger may have encouraged active monitoring of the animations. Detection rates therefore describe awareness under explicit reporting instructions and cannot establish how often drivers would spontaneously notice these changes during everyday use. The animations were also disconnected from road events and driving actions, leaving open whether linking them to the driving situation would have supported interpretation.

The measurement setup introduces further uncertainty. Although participants completed calibration, the panoramic display exceeded the Tobii Pro Spark's optimal screen size, potentially reducing gaze accuracy. The five-press limit also affected both the sample and reporting behaviour: two participants were excluded from all analyses after exceeding it, potentially lowering estimates of false alarms and additional presses. Knowing about the cap may also have discouraged participants from reporting uncertain changes, including early presses, false alarms, and additional presses. Unrestricted reporting could therefore have provided a fuller account of these behaviours.

Comparisons between animations are constrained by differences in exposure, presentation order, and visual design. Participants did not experience every animation, and earlier trials may have influenced later performance. In particular, the red-sphere animation was always the first animation encountered, so its poorer recognition and interpretation cannot be separated from possible learning or familiarisation effects. The animations also differed in colour, movement, timing, and meaning, preventing performance differences from being attributed to any single feature.

Comparisons between onset and completion are further limited by how reports were classified. The onset reporting window was longer than the completion window (60 versus 30~s), allowing more time for an onset report and potentially contributing to detection differences. This also restricted completion reaction times to 30~s, compared with 60~s for onset, so the shorter mean completion reaction time may partly reflect the available reporting time. Moreover, classification relied on trigger-press timing without confirming which change participants had noticed. Some completion reports could therefore have been delayed onset reports, limiting conclusions about participants' ability to distinguish the two events.

Finally, the oral explanations do not provide a fully unaided account of interpretation because they followed multiple-choice questions containing suggested descriptions and meanings. These options may have helped participants formulate their answers, but could also have narrowed or influenced their explanations.

\section{Future research}
\label{sec:futureWork}

Future research could address uncertainty in the visual mappings through repeated workshops and user testing, identifying common associations between information and visual encodings to inform symbol selection and design guidelines. A complementary approach would let participants design their own ambient displays, revealing their preferred themes, symbols and mappings. Comparing user-designed and designer-designed displays over longer periods could establish how familiarity and learnability influence recognition and interpretation.

Another direction concerns the transition from an ambient overview to precise information. Studies could examine how drivers express a need for detail, how the system responds, and whether detailed information should remain visible until dismissed or automatically return to the overview. These interactions should be evaluated during driving to understand their attention demands.

Finally, future work could investigate how a shared visual theme accommodates several pieces of information simultaneously. Combining the sun-to-moon, buildings and red-sphere stimuli would provide one starting point for examining how drivers notice, distinguish and interpret concurrent updates, and whether presenting them together supports broader contextual awareness or makes individual messages harder to understand.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This thesis explored how an aesthetic ambient display on BMW’s Panoramic Vision could communicate non-urgent contextual information through visual metaphors while participants drove in a simulator. Participants often noticed ongoing changes and recognised displayed elements, but had greater difficulty recognising what had changed, identifying when an update had finished and interpreting its intended meaning, showing that noticing a change did not always mean understanding its message.

These findings suggest that aesthetic displays offer a promising way to communicate non-urgent contextual information while driving, with scope to explore a wider range of data through visual metaphors; they also suggest potential for large automotive displays, particularly those at the base of the windscreen, to present peripheral information and support drivers’ contextual awareness, encouraging exploration of richer visual communication beyond ambient lighting.

Beyond communication, aesthetic displays could strengthen manufacturers’ brand identity and shape the sensory character of vehicle interiors, with customisable themes, imagery and information mappings allowing drivers to express their preferences and potentially develop a stronger emotional attachment to the vehicle.

\subsection{Driving Behaviour}
\label{sec:driving-behaviour}

The simulator recorded \texttt{laneOffsetLeft}, \texttt{laneOffsetRight}, and \texttt{laneId} at 100~Hz. The trigger and gaze recordings began in sync, while simulator recording started earlier; because all three streams ended together, we aligned each simulator file to the shared 120-second endpoint using \(t_{\mathrm{aligned}} = \texttt{SimTime} - (\texttt{SimTime}_{\mathrm{final}} - 120\,\mathrm{s})\).

For each eligible sample, we derived the car’s absolute distance from the lane centre as \(d = \left|\frac{\texttt{laneOffsetLeft} + \texttt{laneOffsetRight}}{2}\right|\), calculated the mean distance for each trial and analysis window, and compared trial-level means descriptively across all trials, between animated and non-animated trials, across animation conditions, and between non-animated trials with and without a trigger press.

\begin{evidenceblock}
\normalsize
