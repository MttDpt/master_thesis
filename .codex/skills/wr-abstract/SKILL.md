# Abstract Writing

Load `wr-general` once. Sources: Mensh–Kording rule 5; the CHI notes' hourglass and abstract guidance; Wobbrock's Abstract.

- Tell the complete story of the work, not a description of the document: problem and stakes → approach → main findings → principal conclusion and contribution.
- Begin with the concrete real-world problem and why it matters. Usually one motivation sentence suffices; use two only when an unfamiliar field or key term needs orientation.
- Identify the specific gap before reporting results. State what was done and how the approach addresses it; name the method and study scope at the level needed to understand the findings.
- Report the most informative verified findings, including direction and exact quantities where useful. Replace “many” with a supported count or percentage and its denominator; retain a decisive uncertainty or limitation.
- End by answering the opening problem and naming the delivered contribution and its bounded significance. If the contribution is a framework, name its substantive components.
- Use a punchy, specific finish without unsupported “first”, “only”, or superiority claims; more research is not the principal contribution.
- Treat the abstract as the most widely read standalone account: a reader should understand the problem, method, result, and contribution without opening another section.
- Follow the actual thesis or venue limit. Otherwise, use Wobbrock's default of one paragraph under 200 words; the five-sentence pattern is a scaffold, not a quota.

Useful scaffold: “The problem is … . To address it, [method and scope] … . The main finding was … . This work contributes …, within … .” Add context or findings only when needed; do not invent results to complete the scaffold.
