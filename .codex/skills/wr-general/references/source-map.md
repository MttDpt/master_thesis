# Writing sources and adaptations

Read this source map only when checking provenance, an example, or a conflict between recommendations. Use `wr-general` and the relevant chapter skill for ordinary writing; when reporting or discussing empirical study findings, also read the shared [study-results wording](study-results-wording.md).

## Supplied sources

- **Mensh and Kording, _Ten simple rules for structuring papers_**: supplied Markdown. Rules 2–4 inform the shared reader, context–content–conclusion, logical-flow, parallelism, and consistent-terminology rules; rules 5–8 inform Abstract, Introduction, Results, and Discussion respectively.
- **_How to write a CHI Paper_**, Lennart E. Nacke presentation, 6 May 2019, notes by Jessica Korte: supplied notes. Reader change, the meaning ratio, plain language, hourglass abstract, real-world problem, approach, contribution, and evidence of quality inform the skills.
- **Jacob O. Wobbrock, _Catchy Titles Are Good: But Avoid Being Cute_**, supplied as Wobbrock-2015: supplied Markdown. Abstract, Introduction, Related Work, Design, quantitative/qualitative Method, Results, Discussion, Future Work, and Conclusion inform the corresponding skills.
- The user's accompanying breakdown adds explicit territory → niche → occupying-the-niche and discussion introduction → evaluation → conclusion labels, plus sentence examples; these are included without pretending every phrase occurs in the supplied CHI notes.

## Where guidance lives

| Skill | Source-specific guidance |
| --- | --- |
| `wr-general` | Reader understanding/action; meaning ratio; simple words; context–content–conclusion; no zig-zag; parallelism; stable terms; informative titles. |
| `wr-abstract` | Complete standalone story; problem/stakes, method, specific findings, principal conclusion; name framework components; contribution finish. |
| `wr-introduction` | Territory/niche/response plus Wobbrock's world, problem, approach, findings, contributions; progressive known-to-unknown paragraphs. |
| `wr-background` | Who, what done, what found, how different; thematic teaching and differentiation without superiority. |
| `wr-methodology` | Replicable artefact, principles and trade-offs; quantitative participants/apparatus/procedure/design-analysis; qualitative participants/theory/collection/coding. |
| `wr-results` | Logical chain of supported findings; question/data/answer; independently readable figures; direction and meaning of measured effects. |
| `wr-discussion` | Gap addressed; findings interpreted against literature; explanations, strengths, limitations, and implications. |
| `wr-conclusion` | Deliver Introduction's promises; zoom out to contribution; one or two substantive future directions; end on present value. |

## Adaptation decisions

- Keep motivation brief in the abstract as Wobbrock advises, while retaining the context needed for Mensh–Kording's complete story; use one or two opening sentences according to reader needs.
- Interpret measured direction in Results; reserve mechanisms and wider meaning for Discussion. Explain statistical outcomes only when approved analyses actually contain them.
- Replace the illustrative “clearly show”, “first”, “only”, and causal claims with evidence-calibrated wording. A compelling ending must not manufacture novelty or certainty.
- Use necessary technical terms with plain definitions. Increasing meaning ratio must preserve precision, evidence, reproducibility, and limitations; no artificial score or target.
- Treat five abstract sentences, under 200 words, page allocations, heading preferences, and inline figure placement as adaptable advice. The current thesis/venue template controls actual requirements.
- Do not import obsolete ACM classification/formatting requirements, example study numbers, funding acknowledgements, statistical procedures, grounded-theory labels, or conference-review tactics as facts or obligations.
- Artefact design belongs in the existing Design and Evaluation Methodology skill; future work belongs with Conclusion. Neither requires another routinely loaded skill.

## Optional sentence patterns

- Collection: “The data were collected through [actual method] during [setting/task].”
- Results: “[Outcome] was [observed direction/magnitude] in [defined sample or condition].”
- Interpretation: “One explanation for [finding] is [supported possibility], although [alternative/boundary].”
- Limitation: “[Study feature] limits conclusions about [specific claim or population].”

Use verified content in these patterns; wording such as “we studied the effects of A on B” does not establish causal identification.
